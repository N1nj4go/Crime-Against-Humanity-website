Legal repressions

Legal repression played a crucial role in the persecution of the LGBTQ+ community during the 20th century, as governments used laws to criminalize their identities.

Homosexuality was severely punished in many countries, with penalties ranging from fines and imprisonment to forced labour and even death in some cases. As a result, people lived in constant fear and distress, often forced to hide their identities to avoid arrest or being socialy excluded. Although discrimination against the LGBTQ+ community has existed for centuries, the 20th century marked a period in which these attitudes were reinforced through legal systems.

For example, in 1934, the German Criminal Code: Paragraph 175, was enforced. It aimed to criminalize sexual acts between males, which resulted in the imprisonment of almost 100.000 men who were mainly deported to concentration camps or imprisoned. In total, around 140.000 men were convicted until its abolition in 1994.

Similarly, in the Soviet Union, homosexuality was officially recriminalized under Stalin in 1934 with the addition of the Article 121 to the Criminal Code of the Soviet Union, making relations between men illegal while keeping those between woman legal. Those who were convicted were sentenced to 5 years of hard labour in prisons and were seen as mentally ill.

The repressions were also present in countries such as the United Kingdom with the Labouchère Amendment, who in 1885 criminalized “gross indecency” between men, allowing authorities to prosecute a big range of behaviours. This law remained in force well into the 20th century and led to the arrest and conviction of thousands of men. The Criminal Law Amendment Bill had proposed to criminalise sexual activity between women in 1921, but the law was rejected due to the government categorising lesbianism as not an “urgent problem”, and therefore the law was not applied.
British colonies continued to criminalize same-sex relationships. Laws such as Section 377 of the British Colonial Penal Code criminalised same-sex relationships by defining them as “against the order of nature.” These laws were imposed across colonies in Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean, including countries such as Nigeria, Jamaica, and Singapore. Even after independence, many of these countries kept these laws, continuing to justify arrests and discrimination. Unlike England and Wales that partially decriminalized homosexual acts between two men over the age of 21 in 1967, many former colonies retained these laws until late 20th century.

The “Three-article rule” was an unofficial law used by the police to justify arrests against people who did not dress according to their assigned gender. It was employed from the 1940's until the 1960's and people who did not wear a third piece of clothing considered their gender could get arrested. For example, a woman assigned at birth could be arrested for wearing pants and shirt if they did not wear a third clothing considered feminine.

To conclude, these laws permitted the police to punish citizens without legal constraints.
